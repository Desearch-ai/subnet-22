# Desearch — open network and Bittensor

**Updated:** 2026-09-06

Search expertise exists far beyond one company: people understand different sources, operate infrastructure, train models and develop ranking methods. We want a practical way for that work to improve the product described in our [company foundation](company-foundation.md).

## Why an open network

An open network could help in two distinct ways:

- **Build capacity:** independent operators may add useful source reach and refresh throughput at acceptable full cost. Compare that contribution with competent managed crawling and elastic compute.
- **Method improvement:** outside specialists may discover approaches the team would not develop itself. Once adopted, a useful method can benefit repeated customer requests without its author serving each one.

These need separate evidence. More crawling capacity is not proof of better model research; a useful model may matter even if a particular crawling task does not work well.

The intended relationship is:

**Customer search needs → bounded contribution opportunities → credible evaluation → adopted improvements → customer benefit.**

Problem selection, capable participation and integration determine whether that relationship works. Activity alone does not establish an advantage.

### Alternatives and their tradeoffs

| Approach | Existing strength | What a continuing network is intended to add |
|---|---|---|
| Internal team | Tight coordination, confidential work and accountability | Specialist approaches beyond one hiring plan |
| Open-source collaboration | Shared development, reuse and voluntary or sponsored work | Recurring search problems with explicit evaluation, credit and rewards |
| Bounties or contracted research | Bounded work and straightforward terms | A repeat contribution path across changing problems |
| Managed crawling or elastic compute | Predictable execution of a selected method | Source diversity and competing methods as well as capacity |

These approaches remain useful execution options. The network must justify its coordination and integration costs through outcomes.

## Why Bittensor

Our long-term strategy is to build this contribution path on Bittensor through SN22. Its miner, validator and incentive infrastructure gives us a basis for recurring work and competition across changing search problems, without creating every coordination mechanism ourselves.

That commitment does not mean keeping ineffective tasks forever. We should adapt or stop a workload when its results do not justify continuing, and use what we learn to choose better problems. One unsuccessful task does not settle whether open method discovery can improve search.

Desearch must still choose useful problems, make evaluation credible and operate what reaches customers. Incentives do not establish source truth or customer demand. Other arrangements can support paid research and independent assessment too; our chosen approach must earn trust through useful results.

## Evaluation and decision responsibilities

The proposed contribution path has two distinct outcomes: assessment can inform subnet rewards, while a separate review decides what reaches customers.

```text
Published task or method problem
  → miner output or submitted method
  → assessment against the program's criteria
      → eligible validator weights → Yuma Consensus → protocol reward allocation
      → separate product review → tested search version → customer API
```

This is a conceptual design, not a claim that the new SN22 mechanism is deployed. Eligible validators submit relative weights; Bittensor's consensus and protocol rules govern reward allocation. Completing a task does not imply a fixed payment per page, vector or model. The official [validating](https://www.bittensor.com/docs/guides/validating) and [emissions](https://www.bittensor.com/docs/concepts/emissions) documentation describes those mechanics.

Desearch defines the problems, acceptance rules and participation terms. Miners and builders must identify their outputs, configuration, origins and usage rights. Evaluators produce reproducible evidence; they may be validators or other designated assessors, whose identities, access and conflicts must be clear.

The Desearch team remains accountable for product release and rollback. That review covers rights, search quality, full cost, security and serving constraints. A reward score does not authorize deployment, and consensus alone does not prove source truth, ownership or evaluator independence. Public SN22 records on [taostats](https://taostats.io/subnets/22) make participation inspectable; multiple signatures do not necessarily mean independent checking.

Exploration may be funded before superiority is known, and valid execution work can qualify under its announced terms without inventing a better method. Funding an experiment, recognizing eligible work, continuing a program and deploying a result are separate decisions. A later product decision must not rewrite the completion terms already promised to accepted work.

Evaluation has real compute, refetching, storage, bandwidth and security costs. Program design must make credible checking viable and address copying and concentrated influence.

## What open should mean

These are the principles we intend programs to follow, not a claim that every procedure or reusable asset is already available.

| Area | Commitment and boundary |
|---|---|
| Participation | Discoverable opportunities with stated eligibility, costs and lawful-access requirements |
| Evaluation | Published objectives, methods and material results; private customer material and active holdouts remain protected |
| Credit and decisions | Attribution, reasons and a challenge route; no automatic deployment or permanent reward entitlement |
| Models and code | Develop appropriately licensed open-source search models and aim to make adopted model work openly reusable; code, weights, data and documentation require their own release rights |
| Data and serving | Explain sources, versions and actual checks; public-web access does not automatically allow redistribution, and production access may remain commercial |
| Company operation | Disclose material central control and conflicts; use the same relevant product criteria for internal and external candidates, with blind comparison where practical |

Openness does not require identical permissions for every role. It requires honest disclosure of authority, eligibility and conflicts. The [participation notice requirements](../03-participation-and-progress.md) explain what participants must receive before acting.

## Value flows and rights

The intended lasting benefit is more than a stream of completed jobs: useful search capabilities, visible learning from experiments, credit for contributors and appropriately licensed model/code releases others can reuse. The openness principles above set the limits on that reuse; an operated index is not automatically a public dataset.

These relationships explain the company model, not the full Bittensor token economy:

| Relationship | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Customers → Desearch | Payment for API use or contracted service |
| Protocol → eligible participants | Rewards under applicable protocol and subnet conditions |
| Contributors → product | Accepted data work, models, code, methods and evaluation evidence under stated usage rights |
| Desearch → contributors and ecosystem | Opportunities, feedback, credit and approved reusable releases under program terms |

Eligible work may receive protocol rewards without Desearch issuing company equity. That is neither guaranteed funding nor evidence of lower total cost. Customer receipts, company cash costs, participant costs and protocol incentives must be accounted for distinctly. The official [staking guide](https://www.bittensor.com/docs/guides/staking) explains protocol participation separately.

This company story grants no equity, token entitlement, intellectual-property ownership or right to API revenue. It promises no token price, return or participant profitability. Additional economic commitments require their own explicit terms; protocol-level operations must not be presented as company-funded programs.
